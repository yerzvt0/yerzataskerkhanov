Language & identity
at Nazarbayev University

Kazakh and Russian in everyday student life

NU students move between Kazakh and Russian in everyday conversations. What, if anything, does that mean for how they see themselves?

Same actionA student changes language while speaking with a friend.
Possible meaningsComfort, inclusion, habit, belonging, or something else.

These are possible interpretations, not observed findings of this proposed study.

Research question

Everyday language and identity

How do Nazarbayev University students make sense of their identities through everyday use of Kazakh and Russian?
Population
Nazarbayev University undergraduates who use Kazakh and Russian; participants may differ in fluency and self-identification
Context
Informal peer interaction, such as conversations with friends outside class
Study period
Fall 2026

“Identities” means students’ understandings of self and belonging. It does not presume separate Kazakh and Russian identities.

The question asks about students’ interpretations. It does not assume that speaking a language determines someone’s ethnic or national identity.

Research stance

Why interpretivism fits

The study asks what language choices mean to the people making and experiencing them.

  • Meaning can change with the relationship, setting, and topic.
  • Participants may disagree about the significance of the same practice.
  • The researcher interprets accounts reflexively and remains open to unexpected meanings.

Objective: understand how students connect everyday language use to self-understanding and belonging.

A remembered exchange becomes evidence when a participant explains what happened, who was involved, and why it mattered to them.

Literature · theory

How identity is negotiated

Pavlenko & Blackledge (2004)

Language ideologies and power influence how people position themselves and how others position them. [1]

Bucholtz & Hall (2005)

Identity emerges in interaction; language practices can be intentional, habitual, or interpreted by others in unexpected ways. [2]

Together they support an open question about meaning, rather than assigning a fixed identity to each language.

Literature · students

Research on Kazakhstani students

Zhunussova et al. (2023)

Multilingual identity and language ideologies among 65 graduate students in eight EMI universities. [3]

Zharkynbekova et al. (2025)

A survey and interviews involving 823 university students address language choice and ethno-linguistic identity across Kazakhstan. [4]

These studies already connect student language use and identity. The proposed study builds on them through a narrower interpretive focus.

Literature · practice

Conversation and language context

Kurmanova et al. (2023)

Natural student conversations and interviews in western Kazakhstan show multilingual practices at conversational scale. [5]

Zharkynbekova & Chernyavskaya (2022)

Kazakh–Russian code-mixing can serve communication rather than signal deficient language use. [6]

Smagulova (2008); Kucherbayeva & Smagulova (2023)

Language attitudes, policy, and higher-education conditions provide context for students’ choices. [7] [8]

Contribution

A focused interpretive extension

What meanings do Nazarbayev University undergraduates give to Kazakh–Russian language use in informal peer life?

  • Builds on studies of student identity and multilingual communication in Kazakhstan.
  • Centers participants’ accounts of specific everyday interactions.
  • Explores both identity meanings and practical or habitual explanations.

A focused contribution, without a claim that previous scholarship ignores students or conversation.

The unit of interest is the meaning students give to an interaction, including moments they describe as ordinary and unremarkable.

Analytical framework

Three sensitising concepts

Language choice
Which language is used with whom, when, and how participants explain that choice
Identity negotiation
How students understand themselves and interpret how others see them
Language ideology
Beliefs about what Kazakh or Russian supposedly says about a speaker

A sensitising concept helps guide the interview and interpretation; it does not predetermine an answer.

What happened?How was it understood?What did it mean for belonging?
Expectations and positionality

What I expect, and what I may miss

Provisional expectation: some students may describe context-dependent links between language and belonging.

Alternative: some choices may feel mainly habitual or practical; others may have little identity meaning.

My standpoint: I speak Kazakh comfortably, while observing that some friends have different experiences with it.

I will invite contrasting accounts and keep notes about where my own assumptions influence interpretation.

Evidence and limits

What could answer the question?

Proposed evidence: interviews with Nazarbayev University undergraduates about specific informal conversations.

“Can you tell me about a recent conversation where choosing Kazakh or Russian stood out to you? What did it mean, if anything?”

  • Ask follow-up questions about people, setting, and interpretation.
  • Compare different accounts, including cases with no identity significance.
  • Use voluntary consent and pseudonyms; keep identifiable accounts out of AI tools.

Interviews reveal recollections and interpretations. They do not independently establish every detail of the original exchange.

AI contribution and reflection

What I learned and still need to resolve

AI transparency

I brought the initial topic, question, and personal motivation. My earlier work with AI helped with brainstorming and a source list. AI then helped me check the sources, refine the argument, and draft this presentation.

What working with AI helped me understand, and what I still don’t understand

I now see that a language choice can have an identity meaning or a practical meaning. I still need to think through how interviews can capture everyday conversation without treating remembered accounts as recordings.
